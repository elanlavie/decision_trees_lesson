01 / Decision trees: introduction

Decision trees

A supervised learning method for predicting categories or numbers.

DEFINITIONDecision tree

A model that predicts an outcome by following a sequence of feature-based questions. Each answer selects a branch; the final leaf supplies the prediction.

Input: features

Measurements such as a pet’s height and weight.

Model: learned questions

For example: “Is height ≤ 19 inches?” Training data helps choose the questions.

Output: prediction

A category such as cat/dog, or a number such as journey time.

02 / Tree terminology

Parts of a decision tree

Questions use features. Leaves predict labels. The two thresholds here use different units: 19 inches and 18 pounds.

DEFINITIONNode

A location in the tree: either a question or a prediction.

DEFINITIONRoot

The first node, where every example starts.

DEFINITIONInternal node

A node that asks a question rather than giving the final prediction.

DEFINITIONLeaf

A final node. A classification leaf usually predicts its most common training label.

DEFINITIONBranch

The path from a question to the next node, selected by an answer.

DEFINITIONParent / child

A parent node splits a group; its child nodes receive the resulting groups.

DEFINITIONSplit

Dividing a group of examples according to the answers to a feature question.

DEFINITIONThreshold

The cutoff in a numerical question. In “height ≤ 19,” the threshold is 19 inches.

Connection to KNN and linear regression

KNN predicts from nearby examples’ votes. A decision tree follows learned questions to a leaf. Linear regression predicts a number with a learned line; a regression tree predicts a number within each leaf. All can learn from labeled examples.

What makes a training question useful?

A question divides the examples into two child groups. We want the labels within those groups to be less mixed than before.

DEFINITIONImpurity: a group’s mixing

All one label → pure. A mixture of labels → impure. Gini is the numerical mixing score we will calculate next.

DEFINITIONReduction: a split’s improvement

Compare the parent’s mixing with the size-weighted mixing of its children. A bigger decrease means a better split by this criterion.

05 / Gini impurity and reduction

Calculate impurity, then calculate reduction

DEFINITIONGini impurity

The probability that a randomly selected example from a node would receive the wrong label if we independently assigned it a random label using that node’s class proportions. It measures label mixing within that node.

Count the labels, then find their fractions

A fraction is the number in that class divided by the total number in this group.

Square each fraction, add, subtract from 1

G = 1 − [p(cat)2 + p(dog)2]

G is the group’s impurity.
p(cat) and p(dog) are its class fractions.
p2 means p × p.

What does “randomly labeled” mean?

Imagine a node with 75% cats and 25% dogs. Pick an example at random. Independently guess “cat” 75% of the time and “dog” 25% of the time.

Wrong guesses happen in two ways:
Actual cat + guess dog: 0.75 × 0.25
Actual dog + guess cat: 0.25 × 0.75
Total probability of a wrong random guess = 0.375 = 37.5%.

This thought experiment explains Gini. The real tree predicts the leaf’s majority class; it does not randomly guess this way.

Key characteristics

  • Pure node: G = 0. Every example has the same label. The random-labeling experiment always assigns that label correctly.
  • Two-class range: 0 ≤ G ≤ 0.5. The maximum, 0.5, occurs at a 50–50 mix. More balanced class proportions mean more impurity.
  • More than two classes: for J possible classes, the maximum is 1 − 1/J, reached when all classes are equally common. For three classes, it is 2/3.
  • Role in training: compare candidate splits of the same parent. Prefer the lowest size-weighted child impurity—or equivalently, the largest Gini reduction.
Why squared fractions? What about more classes?

The chance both independent draws are cats is p(cat) × p(cat). The chance both are dogs is p(dog) × p(dog). Add these to get the chance of a match, then subtract from 1 to get the chance of a mismatch.

G(S) = 1 − ∑i=1J pi2

S is the group; J is the number of classes; pi is the fraction in class i. The ∑ sign means “add up the squared fractions for every class.” For J equally common classes, the maximum is 1 − 1/J.

Important: Gini is a label-mixing score, not the error rate of the tree’s majority-label prediction.

DEFINITIONGini reduction (also called Gini gain)

The decrease in impurity caused by a split: parent impurity minus the weighted average of the child impurities. A larger reduction means a better split by this criterion.

The left child contains 5 of the 8 examples, so it gets weight 5/8. The right contains 3 of 8, so it gets weight 3/8.

Weighted child impurity
= (5/8 × 0.32) + (3/8 × 0)
= 0.20

Reduction = before − after

0.50 − 0.20 = 0.30

The split reduced the mixing score by 0.30. This does not mean accuracy increased by 30%.

Why weight by group size? Each training example should count equally. Giving both children half the weight would overemphasize the smaller child.
The general split formula
Gain = G(parent) − [(nL/n)G(left) + (nR/n)G(right)]

n is the parent’s example count; nL and nR are the child counts. The weights sum to 1. This lesson gives each training example equal weight.

TRAINING RULEPrefer the largest impurity reduction

Among the candidate splits being compared at this node, choose the largest gain—equivalently, the lowest weighted child impurity. Then repeat within a child group if another useful split is allowed.

Hands up · Check your reasoning

A split leaves weighted child impurity at 0.50. What is its gain? Did it make the groups less mixed?

Gain = 0.50 − 0.50 = 0. It produces no immediate reduction in mixing by this criterion. A gain of 0.30 is preferable to 0 or 0.17 in our comparison.

Impurity reduction guides training. It is not used to look up the unknown label of a new pet, and a high training gain does not guarantee good performance on new data.

06 / Choosing a feature and threshold

Compare feature–threshold pairs at one node

For an exact search, try every candidate cutoff for each available feature. Choose the pair with the largest Gini reduction.

Use the score: choose one feature–threshold pair at each node

  1. Take only the training examples at this node.
  2. For each available numerical feature (such as height and weight), sort its distinct values and try the thresholds between them.
  3. Score every candidate feature–threshold pair using Gini reduction: parent impurity minus weighted child impurity.
  4. Keep the pair with the largest reduction among the candidates. This chooses one question at this node.
  5. Repeat the search separately in each child with that child’s examples. Stop when a group is pure, a depth/size limit is reached, or no useful split is allowed.
DEFINITIONGreedy training

Choose the best split available at the current node, then repeat locally. The learner does not compare every possible complete tree in advance.

This describes an exact search over numerical splits with all features available. Some algorithms sample features or approximate thresholds. With Gini, call the improvement Gini reduction/gain; “information gain” usually means entropy reduction.

DEFINITIONCandidate threshold

A cutoff between consecutive distinct values of a numerical feature at this node. For heights 10 and 12, use their midpoint 11. Cutoffs within that same gap produce the same split of the training examples.

C = cat · D = dog · Higher gain is better
One node at a time: these tables compare alternative questions for the same current group. After choosing one question, start a new local search in each child that still needs a split. A child may use a different feature—or reuse one.
How do we calculate a candidate’s score?

Gini impurity measures label mixing in one group. Gini reduction = parent impurity − weighted child impurity. Lower mixing after the split means a larger reduction. Chapter 5 showed the calculation; here we use it to compare the candidates.

Equal best gains are possible. A learner uses a consistent tie rule. Here the unrestricted root winner is unique.

DEFINITIONDepth

The number of questions along a path. Maximum depth limits the longest allowed path.

Depth = the most questions along a root-to-leaf path. Pink regions predict cat; blue regions predict dog. Plotted shapes show the training labels.

DEFINITIONOverfitting

Learning details of the training examples that do not generalize well to new examples. Training accuracy may improve while validation performance gets worse.

DEFINITIONUnderfitting

Using a model too simple to capture useful patterns in the data.

Control complexity: cap depth; require more examples per leaf; or prune (remove weak branches from a grown tree). These can reduce overfitting, but too much restriction can underfit. Like choosing K, this is a tradeoff.

13 / Same branches, different leaves

Regression trees predict numerical outcomes

DEFINITIONRegression tree

A decision tree that predicts a number. With the common squared-error objective, each leaf predicts the average of its training outcomes.

Predict a bus journey time

Fictional training times. A leaf average is a prediction, not a guarantee.

Compare with linear regression

A line changes smoothly as a feature changes. A tree gives the same answer within a leaf, then jumps at a boundary.

Regression splits aim to reduce numerical prediction error, rather than class mixing. We do not use Gini for these numerical targets.

14 / Applications

Applications of trees and tree ensembles

Trees are useful for data organized into rows and columns. Real systems often combine many trees.

Online shopping

Which result comes first?

Features could include query match, price, and past engagement. The output is a relevance score used to rank results.

Decision forests can learn ranking tasks. This is not a claim about a particular app’s current model.

Card payments

Does this need a review?

A hypothetical tree might use amount, recent payment frequency, and whether a device is new to flag a transaction.

A false alarm blocks a real purchase. A missed fraud alert lets a bad purchase through. Accuracy alone misses that tradeoff.

School planning

How many meals?

A hypothetical regression tree could use attendance, day of week, and menu to predict cafeteria demand.

Too high means wasted food; too low means students miss lunch. A useful prediction needs a useful decision process.

From tree to forest: a random forest trains varied trees using randomized data/features, then combines their votes or averages. Boosted trees build a sequence that improves earlier predictions. More power can make explanations harder.
Whole-class vote

For a fraud alert, which error feels worse: a legitimate purchase blocked, or fraud missed? What additional information would you need to decide?

15 / Limitations

Interpretable predictions can still be biased

Who gets offered an advanced club?

A school trains a tree using past enrollment. One question is: “Did the student attend the paid summer workshop?”

The tree predicts who will enroll, and the school uses that prediction to decide whom to invite.

Hypothetical scenario. Enrollment is not the same as interest, ability, or who deserves an invitation.

Discuss in groups

  1. Who might the rule overlook?
  2. What might the workshop feature actually reflect?
  3. Suggest one better use of the prediction, or one change to the process.
Workshop attendance may reflect money, transport, free time, or prior access. Historical enrollment may encode unequal opportunity. Consider broad invitations and removing access barriers, and check outcomes across groups. Removing one feature alone may not solve the problem; other features can carry similar information.
Strength: small trees are easy to trace. Limits: trees can overfit, change when training examples change, and reproduce biased patterns. An explanation of a prediction is not evidence of causation.
